# How Often to Add Tubeless Sealant (Hours & Months)

Tubeless sealant needs refreshing every 2–6 months or every 40–60 hours of riding — whichever comes first. Heat, altitude, and dry climates push that window shorter; cool, humid conditions let you stretch it longer. Let sealant go dry and the next thorn that would have self-sealed in seconds becomes a trailside tube installation instead.

![Mountain bike tire on rocky trail showing knobby tread close-up](/images/articles/tubeless-sealant-how-often.webp)

## How Long Does Tubeless Sealant Last?

Most sealants are rated for 2–6 months on the calendar, but calendar time is a rough proxy. Ride hours — the actual hours your tire has been spinning — are a far better unit because they account for the variables that really drive sealant degradation.

A few factors push you toward the shorter end of the 40–60 hour window:

- **Summer heat** — A tire sitting on hot pavement or stored in a 100°F garage loses far more sealant between rides than one kept in a cool basement. Heat accelerates both evaporation and latex breakdown.
- **High altitude** — Lower atmospheric pressure causes liquid to evaporate faster. Riders regularly above 6,000–8,000 feet should check sealant at the 40-hour mark rather than waiting for 60.
- **Dry climates** — Low humidity speeds up latex breakdown. Riders in the desert Southwest typically refresh more often than those riding in the Pacific Northwest.
- **Frequent long rides** — The more miles and hours you accumulate, the more opportunities sealant has to circulate through micro-punctures and escape through the valve stem.

Conversely, cool temperatures, moderate altitude, and shorter rides let you push toward 60 hours or 6 months without issue.

A bike parked for the winter is its own case: sealant left in the tires over a full off-season will dry into a rubbery skin regardless of hours ridden, so don't top it off before storage — plan a full refresh at the start of the season instead. ## How to Check Sealant Condition (The Dipstick Test)

Before buying a new bottle, check how much sealant you actually have. This takes under two minutes:

1. **Lay the bike on its side** with the valve stem pointing up.
2. **Remove the valve core** with a valve core tool. Have a rag ready — sealant will drip if the tire is still pressurized.
3. **Insert a thin spoke, zip tie, or pipe cleaner** through the valve stem until it touches the inner surface of the tire.
4. **Pull it out and inspect** — fresh sealant looks white, milky, or slightly amber and coats the probe. Dried sealant leaves a dry powder or tiny rubber chunks. No coating at all means the sealant is gone.

If the probe comes out clean or powdery, skip the top-up and do a full refresh instead.

## Top-Up vs. Full Refresh: Which Do You Need?

**Top-up**: If sealant is still liquid but running low, add 1–2 oz through the valve stem using a syringe or squeeze bottle. Reinstall the valve core, inflate to ride pressure, and spin the wheel to distribute the fresh sealant evenly.

**Full refresh**: If the dipstick test shows dry residue, or if you're past 60 hours regardless of what the probe shows, pull the tire and start clean:

1. Remove the tire from the rim.
2. Peel out dried sealant residue from the inside of the tire and the rim bed — it comes off in strips once fully dry.
3. Remount the tire tubeless-style and inject fresh sealant through the valve stem (typically 2–4 oz for a 2.3–2.5" MTB tire; 1.5–2.5 oz for a road or gravel tire).
4. Inflate, spin the wheel, and ride a few miles to spread sealant evenly and let it plug any micro-perforations in the casing.

Adding fresh sealant on top of dried chunks reduces effectiveness and can clog the valve core over time. When in doubt, full refresh.

## Signs Your Sealant Has Dried Out

Don't wait for the ride timer — these signals tell you sealant needs attention now:

- **No slosh sound** — Pick up the bike and tilt the wheel side to side. A healthy tubeless tire makes a faint liquid swishing sound. Silence means little or no sealant remains.
- **Flats that won't seal** — A small thorn or staple that should self-plug keeps weeping air. Without active sealant, punctures require manual intervention.
- **Rapid pressure loss between rides** — If the tire drops significantly faster than it used to, dry sealant may have left the casing unprotected or the valve leaking.
- **Heavy white crust at the valve** — Some residue around the valve is normal, but a thick buildup signals sealant is breaking down and migrating rather than doing its job inside the tire.

## What Happens If You Ride on Dried Sealant?

A tubeless tire without active sealant is functionally just a clincher tire without a tube — it rolls fine until something pokes through the casing. The self-healing advantage disappears entirely. Small thorns, wire fragments, and gravel shards that would have self-sealed in seconds instead cause immediate flats requiring a plug, a CO₂ cartridge, or a tube install trailside.

The risk scales with terrain. On smooth pavement, dried sealant may go unnoticed for months. On rocky or thorny singletrack, you'll notice within a ride or two.

## Sealant and Tire Replacement: Check Both Together

Sealant condition and tire condition often degrade on a similar timeline. If you're doing a sealant refresh, take two minutes to check the tire while the rim is exposed — look for squared-off tread, sidewall cracks, and exposed casing threads. For the full checklist, [When to Replace Bike Tires](/articles/when-to-replace-bike-tires) covers all six wear signs and the storage-aging factors that can degrade a tire even when it has low mileage.

Replacing a worn tire without refreshing sealant — or vice versa — is a common half-measure that leaves one of the two problems unresolved.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How often should you add tubeless sealant?

Refresh tubeless sealant every 2–6 months or every 40–60 hours of riding, whichever comes first. Heat, altitude, and dry climates shorten the window; cool, humid conditions extend it. Check with the dipstick method before assuming the sealant is still good.

### How do you check if tubeless sealant is still good?

Lay the bike on its side, remove the valve core, and dip a thin spoke or zip tie through the valve stem to the inner surface of the tire. Milky or white liquid coating means sealant is still active. Dry powder or no residue means it's time to refresh.

### Can you top up tubeless sealant instead of replacing it?

If sealant is still liquid and just low, a 1–2 oz top-up through the valve stem is fine. If sealant has dried into chunks or you're past 60 hours, do a full refresh: remove the tire, peel out dried residue, and start with fresh sealant. Adding liquid on top of dried chunks reduces effectiveness.

### Does tubeless sealant dry out faster in summer?

Yes. Heat accelerates evaporation and latex breakdown significantly. Tires stored in hot garages or ridden in summer conditions may need refreshing at 40 hours rather than 60. Check the dipstick more frequently during warm months.

### What happens if tubeless sealant dries out completely?

The tire loses its self-sealing benefit entirely. Punctures that would have plugged automatically now require manual repair — a plug kit, CO₂, or tube install trailside. Refresh sealant before long rides or remote trails where a flat is harder to manage.

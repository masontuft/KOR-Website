# The 5-Minute Pre-Ride Bike Check (M-Check Guide)

Five minutes before you roll out the door is enough to catch the problems that end rides early — a slow tire, a loose axle, a brake that won't bite. The M-check is the standard framework: it traces a letter M across your bike, hitting every contact point and safety item from front to back. Here's how to run it.

![Cyclist in helmet inspecting bicycle brake and wheel before a ride, ready to go, outdoor natural lighting](/images/articles/pre-ride-bike-check.webp)

## What Is the M-Check?

The M-check is a five-zone walkthrough that follows the shape of the letter M across your bike. Starting at the front axle, you move up through the bars and headset, down through the drivetrain and bottom bracket area, back up to the saddle and post, then down to the rear axle and brakes. Each zone takes under a minute. Together they cover every system that can end a ride or cause a crash.

The M-check is not a substitute for a [proper maintenance schedule](/articles/bike-maintenance-schedule). It tells you whether something is immediately wrong — a cracked tire, a loose axle, a brake that won't engage. It won't flag a chain at 0.5% wear or pads at their 1.5 mm limit. For that, you need component tracking. But for ride safety, the M-check is your last line of defense.

If you just picked up a new bike, start with the [new bike checklist](/articles/new-bike-checklist) — fit, accessories, and tracking setup — then make the M-check your habit before every ride after that.

## The M-Check: Step by Step

### Zone 1: Front Wheel and Axle

Lift the front end and spin the wheel. Watch for wobble (wheel needs truing), flat spots (tire damage), or grinding (bearing issue). Drop the bike and squeeze the front tire — it should feel firm, not spongy.

**Tire pressure check:** See the section below for targets by discipline.

Check the quick-release lever or thru-axle:

- **Quick-release:** The lever should close firmly with moderate hand pressure and leave an imprint on your palm. If it closes too easily or won't seat flush, re-set it.
- **Thru-axle:** Thread it in until snug, then torque to the spec printed on the dropout (typically 12–15 Nm). The axle shouldn't be hand-tight only.

Check both sides of the brake rotor (if disc) or rim surface (if rim brake) for cracks, gouges, or contamination. A rotor with a lip worn into the edge is overdue for replacement — see [when to replace brake rotors](/articles/when-to-replace-brake-rotors).

### Zone 2: Bars, Stem, and Headset

Stand in front of the bike, grip the front wheel between your knees, and try to twist the bars. They should not move relative to the wheel. If they rotate, the stem bolts are loose — stop here and tighten them before riding.

Next, hold the front brake and rock the bike forward and back. Any knocking or clicking from the headset means the lockring or top cap needs adjustment. A loose headset gets worse under load and is dangerous on descents.

Check that brake levers are positioned where you can reach them comfortably — index finger on the lever blade, not doubled over the bar. This matters most if someone else has been on the bike.

### Zone 3: Drivetrain and Bottom Bracket

Backpedal and run through all your gears. Every shift should be clean and quiet. If the chain skips under load, grinds in certain gears, or drops on the front shift, that's a sign the drivetrain needs attention before the ride.

Run your fingers along the chain. A chain that feels gritty or is visibly rusty is past due for a clean and lube — and likely past due for replacement if you haven't measured it. Wet or sandy conditions accelerate wear significantly.

Grab the crank arm and try to wiggle it side-to-side. Any play indicates a loose bottom bracket. Pedal clicks or creaks that appear on every downstroke point to the same problem. Don't ride through a creaking BB — they fail progressively.

Check that both pedals are tight (right-hand thread on the right pedal, left-hand thread on the left). Loose pedals are dangerous and easy to prevent.

### Zone 4: Saddle and Seatpost

Grip the saddle and try to twist it. It should stay put. If it rotates, the saddle clamp bolt needs tightening.

Push down and pull up on the nose and tail — the saddle shouldn't rock forward and back. This can indicate a worn rail or an undertightened clamp.

If you have a dropper post, click it up and down twice. The post should move freely on command and lock solidly when you release the lever. A dropper that creeps down under load or feels sluggish needs service.

Check your seatpost height matches your marker line (a piece of tape or paint mark at your correct height). If you've lent the bike out or knocked it over, it may have shifted.

### Zone 5: Rear Wheel, Axle, and Brakes

Repeat the wheel-spin check from Zone 1 on the rear: spin for wobble, feel for tire firmness, and check the cassette for chipped or hooked teeth. If the chain skips only under high load at the rear, the cassette is likely worn — link to [when to replace cassette](/articles/when-to-replace-cassette).

Check the rear axle the same way as the front. On bikes with a derailleur, also check that the derailleur hanger is straight — a bent hanger causes ghost shifts and can let the chain drop into the spokes at speed.

**Brake test:** Walk the bike forward and squeeze each brake lever hard. The wheel should lock immediately. If you're pulling the lever to the bar before the wheel slows down, your pads are worn, your cable has stretched, or your hydraulics need bleeding. Don't ride until this is fixed.

## Tire Pressure by Discipline

Running the right pressure is one of the fastest performance and safety adjustments you can make, and it takes 60 seconds with a floor pump.

| Discipline | Rider + Bike Weight | Front | Rear |
|---|---|---|---|
| Road (23–25c) | ~155 lb / 70 kg | 85–95 psi | 90–100 psi |
| Road (28–32c) | ~155 lb / 70 kg | 65–75 psi | 70–80 psi |
| Gravel (38–42c) | ~175 lb / 80 kg | 35–45 psi | 40–50 psi |
| MTB XC (2.1–2.25") | ~175 lb / 80 kg | 22–28 psi | 25–30 psi |
| MTB Trail/Enduro (2.4–2.5") | ~185 lb / 84 kg | 18–24 psi | 20–26 psi |

These are starting ranges, not absolutes. Heavier riders run higher pressure; lighter riders go lower. Tubeless setups can run 3–5 psi lower than tubed for the same wheel. If you're hitting pinch flats, go up. If you're washing out in corners, go down slightly and experiment.

A tire that looks fine but has aged rubber (cracked sidewalls, faded color, dried-out feel) is a flat risk regardless of pressure. See [when to replace bike tires](/articles/when-to-replace-bike-tires) for lifespan guidance by type and mileage.

## Quick Brake and Shift Checks

Even if you ran through all five zones, it's worth a 30-second dynamic check before your first descent or intersection:

**Brake feel:** On a flat stretch, squeeze each brake to about 70% and feel for consistency. A spongy or progressive feel on hydraulics (where the lever keeps moving before the bite point) usually means air in the line — get it bled before the next ride.

**Brake squeal on disc brakes:** Occasional squeal is normal in wet conditions. Persistent squeal in dry conditions often means contaminated pads or rotor. Contamination from oil, chain lube, or even sunscreen transfers permanently — you can't wipe it off. Contaminated pads must be replaced.

**Shift check:** While riding at low speed, run the front derailleur through both chainrings (if applicable) and the rear through the full cassette range. Any skipping, rubbing, or hesitation under light pedaling will get worse under load. Index cables stretch after installation and after the winter off-season — a quick barrel-adjuster tweak usually solves it.

## What Each Finding Means (and What to Do)

| Finding | Likely Cause | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Spongy tire | Slow leak or low pressure | Pump up; check for glass or thorn if it recurs |
| Wobbling wheel | Out of true | Ride easy if minor; do not ride if severe — can cause tire rub or crash |
| Loose axle | Undertightened QR or thru-axle | Re-set before riding; do not ride loose |
| Loose headset | Lockring needs adjustment | 5-minute fix; do not ride — it gets worse on descents |
| Soft/fading brake | Worn pads, stretched cable, or air in hydraulic line | Adjust cable or bleed; if pads are worn, replace before riding |
| Chain skip under load | Worn chain or cassette | [Check chain wear](/articles/how-to-measure-chain-wear); replace if at 0.5% or beyond |
| Creaking BB | Loose BB cups or worn bearings | Tighten and re-grease; full BB replacement if it persists |
| Dropper not locking | Worn cartridge or low air | Service the dropper; can usually complete the ride if it's safe to extend manually |

## Pre-Ride Checklist (Copy This)

**Zone 1 — Front wheel:**
- [ ] Tire inflated to target pressure
- [ ] Axle tight (QR firmly closed or thru-axle torqued)
- [ ] Rotor/rim surface undamaged
- [ ] Wheel spins true

**Zone 2 — Bars and headset:**
- [ ] Stem bolts tight (bars don't rotate)
- [ ] Headset tight (no knock when rocking bike)
- [ ] Levers positioned correctly

**Zone 3 — Drivetrain:**
- [ ] Shifts clean through full range
- [ ] Chain clean and lubed
- [ ] No BB side play or creak
- [ ] Pedals tight

**Zone 4 — Saddle and post:**
- [ ] Saddle doesn't rotate or rock
- [ ] Dropper moves and locks (if equipped)
- [ ] Seatpost at correct height

**Zone 5 — Rear wheel and brakes:**
- [ ] Rear tire at pressure
- [ ] Rear axle tight
- [ ] Both brakes lock wheel firmly
- [ ] Rear shift clean; derailleur hanger straight

# Bikepacking for Beginners: Plan Your First Overnighter

Bikepacking doesn't start with a week-long route through the mountains. It starts with one night somewhere close enough that you can bail if things go wrong. The S24O — sub-24-hour overnight — is how most riders actually get into it, and it's the best way to figure out what works before you commit to more.

This guide covers the essential bag setups, route decisions, gear list, and the bike service check you shouldn't skip. Get those right and your first overnighter will be a good trip, not a learning experience.

## Why Start with the S24O

A sub-24-hour overnight means leaving after work on a Friday, sleeping one night out, and riding home Saturday afternoon. You're away for maybe 18 hours total. Distance from home stays under 40–60 miles. If your tent pole breaks, your tire can't hold sealant, or you just hate sleeping on the ground, you're not stranded — you're a short ride or a single rideshare away from your couch.

The S24O forces useful constraints. You can't overpack when you're only out one night and carrying everything on the bike. You learn fast which items earn their weight and which ones you carry once and leave home forever.

Once you've done two or three short overnighters, longer trips get much less intimidating because you've already solved the problems.

## Bag Setups: Start with What You Have

Bikepacking bags mount directly to the bike frame and fork, keeping weight low and centered. A full kit usually includes a seat bag (largest), a frame bag or triangle bag, a handlebar roll, feed bags on the fork legs, and a top-tube bag.

You don't need all of that for a first trip.

**Seat bag** is the most important purchase. A large seat bag (10–16 liters) holds your sleeping bag, bivy or lightweight tent, and layers. Expect to spend $80–$160 for a well-designed one. Avoid ultralight options with no structure — they sway and rub the tire on rough terrain.

**Frame bag** or a basic bikepacking top-tube bag for snacks, phone, wallet, and small tools. If you can't afford a proper frame bag yet, a regular handlebar bag or even a small backpack works for one night. The goal is to test the concept, not run the perfect kit.

**Handlebar roll** comes later. On a first trip, skip it unless you already own one. The extra width affects handling, and you need that figured out before adding loaded bags to both ends of the bike.

Before spending anything on bags, check what you already have. A seat post–mount bag from commuting, a small frame bag, and a jersey pocket takes you further than you'd expect.

## Choosing Your First Route

Loaded riding changes the math on every route. You're carrying 15–25 lbs of gear, your bike handles differently, and hills that were moderate get demanding. Plan for 25–30% fewer miles than your normal day ride for the same effort.

**Aim for mellow terrain.** Loose gravel, chunky singletrack, and technical descents are significantly harder with a loaded rear end. For a first overnighter, paved rail-trails, gravel roads, or smooth doubletrack are better choices than your usual mountain bike loop.

**Campsite first, route second.** Identify your overnight spot before you plan anything else — a dispersed camping area, a state park, a hiker-biker campsite. Build the route around getting there and getting back. Two different routes (out one way, back another) keep things interesting and let you explore more ground.

**Keep the return conservative.** Tired legs, a damp sleeping bag, and morning camp chores add up. Give yourself more time on the return leg than the ride out. If your outbound took 3 hours, plan 4 for the way back.

Use a mapping app designed for cycling to see elevation profiles before you commit. [Cycling route planning apps](/articles/cycling-route-planning-apps) like Komoot let you filter by surface type and calculate estimated moving time with loaded-ride adjustments. Check the full guide to [planning a bike ride](/articles/how-to-plan-a-bike-ride) for more on waypoints, navigation strategy, and backup routes.

## The Bikepacking Gear List

Everything on this list fits in a large seat bag, a frame bag, and your jersey pockets. Total weight typically lands between 14–22 lbs depending on shelter choice.

**Sleep system**
- Sleeping bag or quilt rated for the expected low temperature (go 10°F warmer than the forecast low for your first trip)
- Bivy sack or ultralight tent — a single-wall bivy is lightest and sufficient for one night without rain; a lightweight solo tent adds 1–2 lbs but handles weather and bugs
- Sleeping pad (closed-cell foam pads fold small; inflatable pads save weight but require a patch kit)

**Clothing**
- One extra base layer
- Wind/rain jacket
- Arm warmers — pack these even in summer, because post-sunset and morning descents drop fast
- Gloves for cool mornings

**Food and water**
- Enough food for dinner, breakfast, and snacks
- At least two water bottles — a filter or purification tablets if you're relying on streams
- Solid camp food (instant oatmeal, tortillas, peanut butter, bars) keeps things simple and light

**Tools and spares**
- Multi-tool with chain breaker
- Two spare tubes or tire plugs if you're running tubeless
- Tire levers and mini-pump
- Quick links for your chain size
- Patch kit
- Small roll of gorilla tape wrapped around a lighter

A detailed packing list for day rides and longer trips is in the [what to bring on a bike ride guide](/articles/what-to-bring-on-a-bike-ride). Many of those items carry over directly to overnight trips.

## The Pre-Trip Service Check

Multi-day trips are where deferred maintenance catches up with you. A minor chain wear issue that goes unnoticed on a day ride becomes a slipped gear or a skipping drivetrain halfway through day two with no bike shop nearby.

Do this check at least a week before your trip — not the night before — so you have time to get parts if something needs replacing.

**Chain:** Measure chain wear with a chain wear indicator. At 0.5% stretch, replace it before you go. A worn chain also accelerates cassette and chainring wear, which are expensive to replace. ([How long bike parts last](/articles/how-long-do-bike-parts-last) covers replacement timelines for all drivetrain components.)

**Tires:** Look for embedded debris, cuts, and cracks in the sidewall. Check tread wear. If you're running tubeless, top off sealant before the trip — sealant evaporates over 2–4 months and leaves your tires vulnerable on remote terrain.

**Brakes:** Squeeze hard and check pad thickness. On mountain bikes with disc brakes, a contaminated or glazed rotor makes braking unpredictable on loaded descents. Wipe rotors with isopropyl alcohol if they feel grabby or squealing.

**Drivetrain:** Shift through every gear under light pedaling load. Hesitation, noise, or refusal to shift often means a cable needs tension or a limit screw needs adjustment.

**Headset and stem:** Hold the front brake and rock the bike forward and back. Any knocking or looseness in the headset should be tightened before a loaded overnight.

**Bolts:** Torque-check stem bolts, rack bolts if applicable, and seat post clamp. Vibration from gravel roads backs out fasteners over time.

A full [bike maintenance schedule](/articles/bike-maintenance-schedule) with mileage intervals for every component is worth bookmarking if you haven't gone through it recently.

## Leave No Trace Basics

Bikepacking takes you to places that stay nice only because riders treat them that way.

Camp on durable surfaces: rock, gravel, dry grass. Avoid setting up on vegetation, especially in sensitive alpine or desert zones. Camp at least 200 feet (about 70 paces) from any water source.

Carry out all food waste and packaging. Pack a small trowel for human waste in areas without facilities — cat holes 6–8 inches deep, 200 feet from water and trails, with waste covered and packed out separately.

Stay on the trail. Loading a bike with gear doesn't justify bushwhacking across switchbacks or cutting new lines.

Leave gates as you found them. On agricultural routes, a latched gate means you latch it behind you.

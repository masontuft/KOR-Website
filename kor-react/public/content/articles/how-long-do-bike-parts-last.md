# How Long Do Bike Parts Last? Lifespan of Every Component

Bikes don't announce when parts are worn out — they skip, squeak, or stop, usually at the worst possible moment. Knowing the realistic lifespan of every major component lets you stay ahead of failures, budget for replacements — whether you do the work yourself or book a [shop tune-up](/articles/bike-tune-up-cost) — and squeeze more value out of every ride. Here's a complete reference covering every part KOR tracks, the warning signs that matter more than any mileage average, and why per-rider tracking beats generic intervals every time.

## Component Lifespan at a Glance

These ranges assume regular riding on mixed terrain, typical cleaning habits, and average rider weight around 150–180 lb. Your mileage will vary — literally. Use these as a starting framework, not a hard deadline. Check individual component guides (linked throughout) for the measurement techniques and failure signs that apply to each part.

| Component | Lifespan Range | Primary Failure Sign |
|---|---|---|
| Chain (1x, 11/12-speed) | 1,500–3,000 mi | 0.5% wear on chain checker |
| Chain (8/9/10-speed) | 2,000–3,000 mi | 0.5% wear threshold |
| Cassette | 3,000–6,000 mi (with timely chain swaps) | Shark-tooth or hooked teeth |
| Chainring (road) | 5,000–10,000 mi | Hooked, asymmetric tooth profile |
| Chainring (MTB) | 3,000–6,000 mi | Shark-tooth tips |
| Brake pads — resin | 500–1,000 mi | Under 1.5 mm pad material remaining |
| Brake pads — metallic | 1,000–1,500 mi | Under 1.5 mm pad material remaining |
| Brake rotors | 2,000–4,000 mi (2–4 pad sets) | Under 1.5 mm rotor thickness |
| Road tires (rear) | 1,000–3,000 mi | Squared tread, exposed casing threads |
| Road tires (front) | 1,500–4,500 mi | 30–50% longer than rear |
| MTB tires | 1,500–5,000 mi | Rounded knobs, casing tears, sidewall cracks |
| Tubeless sealant | 40–60 ride hours / 2–6 months | Dried granules on dipstick |
| MTB fork — lower-leg service | Every ~50 hours | Seal weeping, stiction, clunking |
| MTB fork — full damper rebuild | Every 100–200 hours | Blown damper, rebound loss |
| Rear shock | Every 100–200 hours | Oil weeping from shaft |
| Dropper post | Every 100–200 hours | Saddle sag, slow return, side play |
| Bottom bracket | 3,000–10,000 mi | Roughness or play under load |
| Cables & housing | 1,500–3,000 mi / 1–2 years | Fraying, sticky shift or brake feel |

## What Actually Moves the Ranges

The difference between 1,500 miles and 3,000 miles for the same component isn't random — four factors drive most of it.

### Terrain and Conditions

Wet conditions accelerate wear across the board. Water-suspended grit laps against chain links, brake pads abrade faster with mud between pad and rotor, and rubber degrades faster through temperature swings and ozone exposure. Sandy, clay-heavy, or muddy trails are far harder on drivetrains than clean tarmac at the same mileage.

MTB riders typically see half the drivetrain mileage of road riders and a fraction of the tire mileage — not because bikes are worse, but because the loads are different: impacts, lateral stress, and puncture events all extract wear that steady pavement rolling doesn't.

### Rider Weight and Power Output

A 200-lb rider produces more braking heat on a steep descent — faster rotor and pad consumption. A rider who sprints hard loads the chain under peak tension far more often than someone spinning easy gears. Heavier riders and more powerful riders should budget for the low end of every range and inspect earlier.

### Maintenance Habits

This single variable dominates everything else. A clean, lubed chain on a 2,500-mile timeline easily beats a grimy, dry chain that hits 0.5% stretch at 1,200 miles. Letting a chain stretch to 0.75% or 1.0% before replacing it accelerates wear on cassette and chainrings — parts that cost three to five times more to replace than the chain itself.

The same logic runs through the whole bike: a fork with fresh oil at 50 hours outlasts one that never gets serviced until the seals blow. Regular upkeep shifts every part toward the top of its range.

### Riding Volume and Pace

High mileage compressed into short calendar windows doesn't give components time to dry between sessions. Riders covering the same total miles over a longer season often see longer-lasting consumables because conditions vary more and the bike dries out between rides. If you're riding through every wet season, plan for the low end of every range and inspect more frequently.

## Chain: The Part That Controls Everything Else

The chain is the most cost-effective maintenance point on the bike. Replace it before 0.5% stretch, and your cassette, chainrings, and derailleur pulleys all last significantly longer. Wait until 0.75–1.0%, and you've accelerated wear on components that cost three to five times more.

11- and 12-speed chains are narrower and reach 0.5% faster than older 8/9/10-speed setups. If you're running a modern groupset, plan for the shorter end of the range — especially in wet or gritty conditions.

## Cassette and Chainrings: Chain Timing Determines Everything

Replace your cassette every 2–3 chains and it will outlast several chain cycles without showing meaningful shark-tooth wear. Run a worn chain too long, and the cassette teeth file down to match the stretched pitch — you'll know because a new chain immediately skips under load.

The rule most mechanics follow: if the cassette teeth have visible hooked or asymmetric profiles, replace cassette and chain together. Never put a new chain on a worn cassette if you're seeing skipping; you'll destroy the new chain within a few hundred miles.

Chainrings outlast cassettes by a wide margin — typically two to three cassette replacements — because they carry less cumulative wear per rotation. MTB 1x chainrings take more abuse than road rings due to cross-chaining loads and debris. See [when to replace your cassette](/articles/when-to-replace-cassette) for the visual tooth check and the shark-tooth test.

## Brake Pads and Rotors: Check by Measurement, Not Sound

Brake pads are the easiest check on the bike: if there's under 1.5 mm of material left, replace them. The challenge is that worn pads are nearly silent until metal contacts rotor — and by that point you've scored the rotor surface and likely need a rotor replacement too.

Resin pads last 500–1,000 miles; metallic sintered pads last 1,000–1,500 miles. Descending-heavy routes, steep commutes, or wet conditions push you toward the low end. Some riders in wet climates replace resin pads every 400–600 miles without a single complaint from the brakes.

Rotors last roughly 2–4 pad sets, or 2,000–4,000 miles. The retirement threshold is 1.5 mm rotor thickness. Below that, heat management degrades significantly and the rotor can crack under sustained hard braking. You can measure with a digital caliper at the braking surface — any bike shop can also check this in under two minutes.

For the 1.5 mm measurement technique and the sound-based early warning signs, see [when to replace bike brake pads](/articles/when-to-replace-brake-pads).

## Tires: Six Signs That Beat Mileage Estimates

Road tires typically last 1,000–3,000 miles on the rear and 30–50% longer on the front because the rear takes most of the weight and braking load. MTB tire wear is less about mileage and more about knob condition and casing integrity — a rocky enduro trail chews knobs faster than a smooth flowy singletrack at the same total distance.

The six reliable retirement triggers for any tire:

1. **Squared tread** — a visible flat center section when viewed from the side
2. **Exposed casing threads** — white or beige fibers visible through worn rubber
3. **Sidewall cracking** — UV-induced brittleness, or deep gashes more than a few millimeters
4. **Frequent flats** — the casing is failing to protect the tube or sealant
5. **Persistent bulges** — casing failure that can't be repaired
6. **Knob rounding (MTB)** — center knobs worn flat enough to lose traction in loose conditions

For the full breakdown by discipline — road, MTB, e-bike — see [when to replace bike tires](/articles/when-to-replace-bike-tires).

If you run tubeless, sealant needs refreshing every 40–60 ride hours or 2–6 months, whichever comes first. Sealant that has dried into granules can no longer seal punctures — check it on the same schedule you clean your drivetrain.

## Suspension: Hours, Not Miles

Suspension service intervals are measured in hours, not mileage, because compression cycles depend on terrain and saddle time rather than distance covered. A shuttle-lapping bike gets more suspension work per mile than a smooth trail bike at the same mileage.

The standard service schedule:
- **Lower-leg service** (clean seals, replace bath oil): every ~50 ride hours
- **Full damper rebuild** (all internals, IFP reset, all seals): every 100–200 hours

A neglected fork doesn't degrade gradually and obviously — it feels normal, then suddenly feels broken. Regular lower-leg service keeps contaminants out before they score the stanchions. Scoring is what turns a $150 service into a $400–600 rebuild.

Rear shocks follow the same 100–200 hour full-service interval. Oil weeping from the shock shaft is the primary visual warning sign.

For brand-specific intervals (Fox vs. RockShox), symptom diagnosis, and what a lower-leg service actually involves, see [MTB suspension service intervals](/articles/suspension-service-intervals).

Dropper posts use the same hour framework. Service every 100–200 hours — symptoms are saddle sag under body weight, a slow return stroke, or lateral play at the saddle head.

## Bottom Bracket: Longest-Lasting, Hardest to Diagnose

The bottom bracket is the least-serviced part on most bikes and, paradoxically, one of the easiest to ignore for too long. A healthy BB spins smoothly with no detectable side-play when you grab the crank arms and push-pull laterally.

Lifespan ranges from 3,000 to 10,000 miles depending primarily on:
- **BB standard**: Threaded BSA/T47 generally outlasts press-fit because the interference fit is consistent and doesn't flex. Press-fit BBs in frames with even slightly out-of-spec tolerances allow micro-movement that grinds bearings rapidly.
- **Weather exposure**: Road salt and regular wet riding knock bearings toward the low end of the range.
- **Shell sealing**: A well-sealed shell (thread-locker or grease on threads, proper preload) keeps contamination out for much longer.

The most common BB complaint — a creak with every pedal stroke — often isn't the bearings at all. Pedal threads, chainring bolts, the seatpost clamp, or the headset produce the same pedaling-rhythm creak. Rule those out before pulling the BB. For the full elimination process and the spin/play tests that confirm a worn BB, see [bottom bracket creaking: diagnosis & replacement guide](/articles/bottom-bracket-creaking).

## Cables, Housing, and Contact Points

Cables and housing don't get a lot of attention, but a frayed cable or crushed housing creates shifter slop and spongy brake feel that can't be fixed by adjustment alone. Replace cables and housing every 1,500–3,000 miles or every 1–2 years, whichever comes first. Riders in salty coastal or winter environments often replace them annually.

Bar tape and grips are cosmetic items that become functional concerns when they compress set or slip. Silicone and foam grips compress noticeably after 1,500–2,000 miles; leather bar tape can go longer but stiffens over time. Replace when they stop absorbing road buzz or when your hands slip on descents.

## Why Averages Don't Tell You Where You Are in the Range

Here's the problem with the table at the top of this article: it's built on fleet-level averages across thousands of different riders on thousands of different bikes. It tells you what's typical — it doesn't tell you where you personally sit in the range.

A road rider who commutes on clean pavement all year will hit the top of every range. A mountain biker who shuttles through every wet season will hit the bottom. Same recommended service interval. Completely different real-world outcomes.

The mileage check that actually works is tracking wear by *your* actual mileage — not a calendar, not a community average. When you know exactly how many miles are on your current chain, how many hours your fork has seen since its last lower-leg service, and when your brake pads were last measured, you know precisely where you stand in the range.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How often should I replace my bike chain?

Replace it when a chain checker reads 0.5% wear — before it reaches 0.75%. At typical recreational riding volumes, that's every 1,500–3,000 miles for modern 11/12-speed chains. Wet conditions and heavy loads push you toward the lower end.

### Can I put a new chain on a worn cassette?

In the short term if necessary, but a new chain on a worn cassette will skip under power because the teeth have worn to the old chain's stretched pitch. If the cassette shows hooked or asymmetric teeth, replace both chain and cassette at the same time.

### How do I know when my brake pads are worn out?

Measure the remaining pad material — anything under 1.5 mm means immediate replacement. You can also catch wear early by feel: a longer lever stroke that doesn't improve after bleeding, or a squealing that appears at light braking pressure. Never wait for the metal-on-metal scraping sound; that means the rotor is being scored.

### How long do bike tires last in storage?

Rubber degrades from UV exposure, ozone, and temperature cycling even without use. Tires older than 5–6 years should be inspected for sidewall brittleness and micro-cracking before you trust them on a ride, even if the tread looks fine. Store tires in a cool, dark, and dry place to maximize shelf life.

### Does e-bike riding wear parts faster?

Yes. E-bikes run higher average speeds and produce higher drivetrain loads on acceleration, which wears chains, cassettes, and chainrings 20–40% faster than a comparable human-powered ride at the same mileage. Use e-bike-specific chains where available, and check wear more frequently.

### How long does a bottom bracket last?

Threaded BBs in good conditions can last 5,000–10,000 miles. Press-fit BBs in frames with tight tolerances often land in the 3,000–6,000 mile range. The biggest variable is whether the shell is well-sealed and whether you're riding in wet or salty conditions regularly. Check for roughness and play annually regardless of mileage.

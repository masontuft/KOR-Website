# How to Store a Bike for Winter (Without Ruining Parts)

A bike that sits all winter doesn't need a heated garage — it needs to go in clean, dry, and with a plan for what happens the moment you take it back out. Skip the prep and you'll find a rusty chain, flat-spotted tires, or a dead sealant layer in the spring. Here's how to store a bike for winter the right way, in the order it actually matters.

## 1. Wash and Lube Before It Sits

Road grime left on a bike over months turns corrosive — dirt holds moisture against metal, and that's how a chain rusts even indoors. Give the bike a full wash, dry it completely, and relube the chain before it goes into storage. A dry, lubed chain sitting still is fine; a dirty, damp one is what causes pitting you'll feel as roughness or hear as a squeak the first time you ride it again.

While you're at it, wipe down the frame and check for any drivetrain wear worth addressing now rather than after months of neglect. The [bike maintenance schedule](/articles/bike-maintenance-schedule) is the reference for what's due — it's easier to replace a marginal chain or cassette before winter than to discover it's shot on the first ride of spring.

## 2. Set Tire Pressure — and Get the Bike Off the Tires If You Can

Tires left at riding pressure for months are fine; tires left flat are not. Either way, top off pressure to near the tire's max rating before storage — this resists flat-spotting and slows the natural pressure loss that happens over weeks of sitting.

Better still: get weight off the tires entirely. Hanging the bike from a wall hook or ceiling hoist, or setting it on a stand that lifts both wheels, prevents flat spots from forming where the tire's been compressed against the floor in one spot for months. If floor storage is your only option, roll the bike forward every few weeks to shift the contact patch.

Tire rubber also ages in place, storage or not — see [when to replace bike tires](/articles/when-to-replace-bike-tires) for the cracking and dry-rot signs that mean a tire's done regardless of tread depth.

## 3. Plan for Sealant to Dry Out

If you're running tubeless, don't expect the sealant to survive a full winter. Sealant is designed to stay liquid through evaporation cycles that assume regular riding; parked for months, it dries into a rubbery skin inside the tire and stops sealing anything. Don't top it off mid-winter — just plan a full sealant refresh at the start of the season, before your first ride, rather than discovering it's dead when a small puncture doesn't seal. See [tubeless sealant: how often to refresh](/articles/tubeless-sealant-how-often) for what a proper refresh involves.

## 4. Pull or Trickle-Charge Batteries

Any battery on the bike needs attention before storage, not after. Lithium batteries — lights, SRAM AXS shifting, e-bike packs — self-discharge slowly even when off, and a battery left fully drained for months can degrade permanently or fail to hold a charge at all.

- **Lights**: remove them from the bike and store around 50% charge if the device allows it; don't leave them mounted and dead all winter.
- **AXS batteries**: pull them off the derailleurs and shifters and store at partial charge; a coin-cell shifter battery can generally just stay in place. See [SRAM AXS battery life](/articles/sram-axs-battery-life) for how long a charge actually lasts and the signs it's fading.
- **E-bike packs**: follow the manufacturer's storage charge level (commonly 40–60%) and check on it monthly — never store a lithium pack fully empty or fully full for extended periods.

## 5. Mind Where It's Actually Stored

Not all "indoors" is equal. A heated basement or living space is close to ideal — stable temperature, low humidity. An unheated shed or detached garage swings with the weather outside and often runs damp, which is exactly the environment that rusts chains and pits bearings even on a bike that was clean going in.

If a shed or garage is the only option, a breathable cover keeps dust and condensation off without trapping moisture the way a sealed plastic bag would. A silica gel packet or two near the bike helps in genuinely humid spaces. What you're avoiding either way is trapping damp air directly against bare metal for months at a stretch.

## 6. Run a Spring-Readiness Check Before the First Ride

Don't just wheel it out and go. A five-minute check before the first real ride catches anything storage caused:

- Squeeze both brake levers and confirm firm, consistent bite.
- Check tire pressure and inspect for dry rot or cracking along the sidewall.
- Spin the cranks and wheels, listening for any new roughness or drag.
- Confirm lights and any electronic shifting power on and hold a charge.
- Top off or fully refresh tubeless sealant per the schedule above.
- Give the chain a fresh lube pass even if it looks fine — winter oxidation isn't always visible.

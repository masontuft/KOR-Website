# New Bike Checklist: 10 Things to Do After You Buy

A new bike is ready to ride, but it isn't ready to be *yours* until you've dialed in fit, gathered a handful of essential accessories, and put a few minutes into setup that pays off for years. None of this takes more than an afternoon. Here's what to do first, in the order it actually matters.

## 1. Get Your Fit Basics Right

Saddle height is the fastest fix with the biggest payoff: with the pedal at the bottom of its stroke and your heel on it, your leg should be almost straight — that translates to a slight bend in the knee once your foot is clipped in or on the pedal properly. Too low strains your knees over time; too high makes you rock side to side and lose power.

Reach matters next. Sitting on the saddle with hands on the bars, your elbows should have a soft bend, not a locked-out stretch or a cramped bunch-up. A shop fit appointment (often free or cheap with a new bike purchase) catches reach and saddle angle issues a few minutes at home won't — worth booking in the first couple of weeks before bad habits or discomfort set in.

## 2. Buy Accessories in Priority Order

You don't need everything on day one. In order of what actually keeps you riding safely:

- **Helmet** — non-negotiable, first purchase if you don't already have one.
- **Flat kit** — spare tube (or plug kit for tubeless), tire levers, and a mini pump or CO2 inflator. See [what to bring on a bike ride](/articles/what-to-bring-on-a-bike-ride) for the full list once you're past the basics.
- **Lights** — even for daytime riders, a rear light adds visibility that's cheap insurance.
- **Lock** — sized to how often the bike will be left unattended in public.
- **Multi-tool** — covers minor on-bike adjustments (saddle, bars, derailleur limit screws) without a full toolbox.

Everything past this tier — computer, extra bottles, bags — is nice-to-have, not need-to-have.

## 3. Register Your Serial Number

Every bike has a serial number, usually stamped on the underside of the bottom bracket shell. Photograph it and register it with a bike registry (many are free) the same week you buy the bike, not after something happens to it. A registered serial number is one of the few things that makes a recovered stolen bike actually traceable back to you, and some insurance claims ask for it directly.

## 4. Use Your Free 30-Day Shop Check

Most shops include a free tune-up or safety check within the first 30 days of a new bike purchase — it's built into the price whether you use it or not. New cables stretch and new spokes settle in the first few hundred miles, so this checkup catches the minor creaks, slight brake rub, or shifting drift that's completely normal on a bike that's still breaking in. Skipping it just means those small issues sit unaddressed instead of getting a five-minute fix.

The easiest time to start tracking maintenance is before anything has worn down, not after something breaks. ## 6. Learn the M-Check

The M-check is a 30-second pre-ride habit, not a maintenance chore — trace an M shape over the bike, checking as you go: front wheel (quick release tight, tire pressure), fork and brake, headset and stem, saddle and seatpost, bottom bracket and cranks, rear brake and derailleur, rear wheel (quick release, tire pressure). Doing it before every ride catches a loose bolt or soft tire before it becomes a roadside problem. Pair it with a full [pre-ride bike check](/articles/pre-ride-bike-check) on rides that matter more, and you'll rarely get caught out.

## Building the Long-Term Habit

None of these steps are one-time — fit gets rechecked as you log miles, accessories get replenished, and tracking only pays off if it keeps running.
